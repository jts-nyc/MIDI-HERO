## What the sampled evidence can and cannot support

The reliable visual result is a hierarchy of anticipation, localized strike feedback, and slower cumulative state, with a visible loss/rebuild example and bounded tutorial goals. These recordings do not isolate the effect of that hierarchy on motivation. They do not verify specific engine thresholds or permit precise timing measurement.

The V2 sequence is the most useful short behavioral case: a prior multiplier disappears, the song presentation continues, and a lower multiplier later appears. It supports separating *continuing to play* from *recovering the old scoring advantage*. It does not establish the immediate sound consequence of the error. The report uses this as visual corroboration of a documented scoring contract, not a reconstructed input trace.

The V1 controller composite confirms that such footage is available and useful, but finger visibility is not a substitute for digital button logging. Presses may be occluded, repeated fret holds may look static, and independently recorded layers may have offsets. A clean-looking hand camera cannot certify a millisecond hit window.

## The next targeted capture, if original gameplay is available

Use one short, low-density passage with a clear foreground part. Record game audio/video and a raw input overlay with their offsets characterized. Repeat it correctly, with one omitted target, one wrong fret, one empty strum, one early release, and a correct recovery after each. Keep title, platform, build, chart, difficulty, calibration, and audio mix fixed. Compare the first changed frame, sound behavior, target consumption, score/multiplier and health—not just the final percentage. Then repeat the genuinely version-dependent cases on another title.

This would resolve more of the remaining feedback uncertainty than accumulating additional expert full-combo videos. Until then, the report's audio and fine-grained original-engine claims remain explicitly bounded by their documentary sources.
